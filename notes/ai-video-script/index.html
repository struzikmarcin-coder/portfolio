How to Write an AI Video Script People Watch to the End

Where AI genuinely speeds up writing a video script, the three-question brief that keeps it from sounding generic, and the one decision that still has to come from a person.

Ask any AI model for a “video script about our new feature” and you get the same thing back, almost word for word: “Introducing the all-in-one solution that helps you…” It reads like release notes with a voiceover attached, and most viewers are gone before the second sentence. That is not the model failing. It is doing exactly what it was asked: write about the feature. Nobody asked it to write about the person watching.

The problem with AI-written scripts

A feature brief describes what was built. A script that holds attention describes what changes for the viewer. Those are different documents, and feeding the first one straight into a prompt produces exactly the generic, feature-first copy everyone has learned to skim past. The fix is not a cleverer prompt — it is changing what goes in before you ask for anything.

The three-question brief

Before I ask AI for a single line, I answer three questions myself and paste them in as the brief:

  • Who is watching, and what were they doing five minutes before this video? Not a persona name — the actual task they were mid-way through.
  • What was annoying or slow before this feature existed? The specific friction, not the category of problem.
  • What is the one thing they should remember if they forget everything else? If the answer is more than one sentence, it is not ready yet.
Needs your input: these are the three questions I think you'd ask — replace with your actual brief if it's different, since this is the part readers will want to copy.

Getting five hooks and keeping one

With that brief as context, I ask for five different opening lines, not one. Most AI output regresses to the mean on the first try; asking for variation and then choosing is far more reliable than asking for perfection once. Four of the five get thrown away. That is normal, and it is faster than editing one mediocre line into a good one.

What stays human

The one decision I never hand over is which single idea the video is built around. AI is good at generating options and reorganizing a mess into a structure; it is not good at knowing which option matters most to a specific audience at a specific moment, because that judgment comes from sitting in on customer calls and watching which features people actually struggle to explain to their own teams. Handing that decision to a model flattens every video into the same shape.

A before/after example

“Introducing our new AI Course Creator, the all-in-one solution that helps you build and sell online courses faster than ever.”

That is a typical first-draft AI output from a plain feature brief: generic, feature-led, could be about any product in the category. The version that shipped led with the specific moment of friction — staring at a blank course outline with no idea where to start — before the product appeared on screen at all.
